2. Component A: Prose Section

Statistical analysis indicates that three specific prose pieces dominate the examination.

2.1 The Model Millionaire (Oscar Wilde)

Probability: 95%
Sure Shot
Q: Give a brief character sketch of Baron Hausberg.
Introduction

Baron Hausberg is one of the most pivotal characters in Oscar Wilde's story 'The Model Millionaire'. He serves as a fascinating study in contrasts—a man of immense wealth who retains a deep sense of humanity. He is the richest man in Europe, yet he possesses a playful and observant nature that sets him apart from the typical cold-hearted aristocracy.

The "Millionaire Model" vs. "Model Millionaire"

Wilde uses the Baron to create a clever paradox.
• As a Millionaire Model: He poses as a beggar for his friend Alan Trevor. This eccentricity shows his love for art and his desire to experience life from different perspectives. He is comfortable sitting in rags despite being able to buy the whole studio.
• As a Model Millionaire: He embodies the ideal (model) behavior of a wealthy person. Instead of being arrogant, he uses his wealth to help others. He proves that true nobility lies in action, not just in status.

A Sharp Observer of Human Nature

During his interaction with Hughie Erskine, the Baron is silent but highly observant. He notices Hughie's genuine distress and his act of selfless charity. Unlike others who might have been offended by being given a copper coin, the Baron has the emotional intelligence to see the kindness behind the act. He is not a passive figure; he is actively studying Hughie's character.

Benevolence and Generosity

The climax of the story reveals the Baron's true nature. He rewards Hughie not just with money, but with a future. By sending the cheque for £10,000, he solves Hughie's life problem. His generosity is not impulsive but calculated—he rewards "goodness" with "fortune." This act elevates him from a mere financier to a fairy-godfather figure.

Conclusion: In essence, Baron Hausberg represents the theme that "Millionaire models are rare enough, but model millionaires are rarer still." He is a man who combines the power of wealth with the grace of kindness.

2.2 The Tell-Tale Heart (Edgar Allan Poe)

Probability: 95%
Q: Discuss the significance of the title "The Tell-Tale Heart".
Introduction

The title 'The Tell-Tale Heart' is a masterstroke of psychological symbolism. It refers to a heart that "tells a tale"—specifically, the tale of a crime that the narrator desperately tries to conceal. The title encapsulates the central theme of the story: that guilt cannot be silenced, no matter how perfectly a crime is executed.

The Symbolism of the Heartbeat

Throughout the story, the heartbeat serves two functions:
• External Reality: Initially, the narrator claims to hear the old man's heart beating due to "terror." He describes it as "a low, dull, quick sound, such as a watch makes when enveloped in cotton."
• Internal Guilt: After the murder, the sound returns. Since the old man is dead, the beating heart cannot be his. It is, in fact, the narrator’s own heart, pounding due to adrenaline and guilt. However, in his madness, he projects this sound onto the dead body.

The Irony of the Telling

The word "Tell-Tale" implies a betrayal. The heart betrays the narrator. He had planned the perfect murder—dismembering the body and hiding it under the floorboards—but his own conscience (symbolized by the heart) forces him to confess. The louder the heart beats, the more his sanity crumbles, until he screams, "It is the beating of his hideous heart!"

Conclusion: The title is apt because the heart acts as the ultimate witness. It tells the tale of the narrator's descent from calculated madness to uncontrollable guilt, proving that one cannot escape one's own conscience.

2.3 Prayer (Mahatma Gandhi)

Probability: 90%
Q: What is the need and value of prayer according to Gandhi?

Mahatma Gandhi considers prayer to be the very essence of human life. For him, prayer is not a religious ritual but a spiritual necessity.

Food for the Soul: Gandhi uses a powerful analogy to explain the need for prayer. He argues that just as the body needs food to survive, the soul needs prayer for its nourishment. A man can live without food for days, but he cannot live without prayer if he wants to keep his spirit alive. Without prayer, the soul becomes weak and "dead."

A Petition for Strength: Gandhi clarifies that prayer is not asking God for worldly favours. Instead, it is a "longing of the soul," a "confession of our weakness," and a "petition for inner strength." It is a way to clean our hearts of malice and selfishness.

Universal Discipline: Finally, Gandhi emphasizes that prayer brings discipline to life. It belongs to no single religion; it is the universal language of truth. It allows a person to connect with the divine and find peace in a chaotic world.

3. Component A: Poetry Section

Sigh No More, Ladies (William Shakespeare)

Q: Summarize the central theme of the poem.

Introduction: This poem is a song from Shakespeare’s play Much Ado About Nothing. It is a lighthearted yet cynical commentary on the nature of men and the resilience of women.

Men as Deceivers: The poet begins by advising women not to "sigh" or mourn over men. He states that men are "deceivers ever," meaning they are naturally unfaithful. They have "one foot in sea and one on shore," symbolizing their inability to stay constant or committed to one person/place. Their love is fleeting and unreliable.

The Advice to Women: Instead of wasting their youth and beauty crying over such fickle men, Shakespeare advises women to "let them go." He urges women to transform their "sounds of woe" into "Hey nonny, nonny"—a joyful, carefree song. The poem empowers women to accept male infidelity as a natural fact and move on to live a happy, independent life.

On Receipt of My Mother’s Picture (William Cowper)

Q: Discuss the poet's feelings upon receiving his mother's picture.

The Power of Memory: Cowper wrote this elegy fifty years after his mother's death, upon receiving her portrait. The picture acts as a powerful catalyst, bringing back "faithful remembrances." He feels as though his mother has come back to life, and he impulsively kisses the canvas.

Childhood Nostalgia: The poem vividly reconstructs his childhood. He remembers the "biscuit or confectionary plum" she used to give him, her nightly visits to his room, and the comfort of her presence. He contrasts his own life, which has been a "tempestuous sea" (stormy and difficult), with her presence which was a "safe shore."

Conclusion: The poem is a touching tribute to maternal love. Cowper concludes that while time has taken her away physically, the picture has preserved her spirit, proving that love transcends death.

Strategic Exam Execution Guide

1. "Translation Hack": Do not translate word-for-word. Focus on flow. Use elevated vocabulary (e.g., instead of "Books are tower of light", write "Books serve as a beacon of light").

2. Structural Headings: For Character Sketches, NEVER write a single block of text. Use headings like Introduction, The Irony, Benevolence, Conclusion.

3. Managing Choice: In Section A, always choose The Model Millionaire question. It is factual and easier to score high on than the abstract Tell-Tale Heart.
